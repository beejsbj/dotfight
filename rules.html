Dawood made this game up at school. He and Burooj played it in grades 5 and 6, with ballpoint pens, on the back pages of their exercise books. Nobody wrote the rules down. Twenty years on, here they are: remembered, argued over, and written out properly.

This book is Dawood's game with its core rules: everything you need to play, and what the game on this site plays.

The page

Two players, a blue pen and a red one, and one sheet of paper. Everything drawn stays drawn. The dead are crossed out, never rubbed out, so a finished war is a page full of lines and crosses.

Setup

  1. Take turns drawing bases (circles), one at a time, and jot each one full of soldier dots.
  2. Positioning. Before anyone flicks, each side arranges its soldiers: anywhere inside its bases, or a little outside the walls 20 units, a dot and a bit. The first player arranges first, and the second gets to see it.

Nobody has to stay in, but it pays. Walls protect: a snipe loses power going through one and a lunger's hand shakes. And a lunger who lands among your soldiers gets shot.

A turn

one flick (a snipe or a lunge)+ one free send

Flicking

Pull back and let go, like flicking a pen stood on its tip. The harder the flick, the longer the line, and the wilder. A soft flick is short and sure.

Snipe

Your soldier stays put and the ink does the work. The line crosses out every enemy soldier it crosses. It goes straight through them.

Power loss. The paper fights back. Going through a base wall costs the line a lot of its power; going through a soldier costs less. The line thins and falls short. to test 10% of what's left at a wall, 5% at a man; the wall at your back is free

Two with one bullet. Cross out two or more with one line and you flick again. It can chain, and power loss is what stops it: a line that pays for every wall and every man can't take many. to test

Lunge formerly “move”

Your soldier runs along his own ink, as far as a shot can go, and crosses out every enemy he runs through. He stands where the ink stops.

  • Walls shake him. Crossing a wall shakes his hand; crossing a soldier shakes it a little. It's the snipe's power loss, paid in shake instead. to test about 4.6° at a wall, 2.3° at a man
  • Lunge again. Each kill earns another lunge by the same soldier, a little shakier every time +0.05 rad, about 3°, a link. You can always stop.
  • Where he lands decides. Inside an enemy base with enemy soldiers in it, they shoot him on the spot. In an empty enemy ring nothing happens: there's nobody left to shoot him (and if he crossed out the last man inside on his way in, there's nobody by the time he lands). Off the page, he's gone. Whoever he crossed out on the way stays crossed out.
  • Through a base. He may cut right through an enemy base, crossing out the soldiers inside, as long as he doesn't land in it. He pays shake at both walls and at every man he crosses: costly, but legal.

Send

Once a turn, and free: send up to 5 soldiers walking from one of your bases to another, an empty ring included. Send before your last flick. to test

On the road for one turn. The convoy walks out in plain sight as the pen changes hands, a quick time-lapse between turns, and arrives at the start of your next turn. That leaves exactly one enemy turn in which it's on the open paper, and any line that crosses it then crosses them out. to test

Why five: a big convoy on open paper is exactly what a two-with-one-bullet snipe is looking for, so the size of a send keeps itself in check. In the long war, convoys walk for several turns instead (see book 2).

Bases

An emptied base doesn't vanish. It stays on the page as an empty ring, and a send can man it again. Until then an enemy lunger can land in it safely.

Walls have friction: snipes lose power through them and lungers shake.

Last stand

When a side is down to its last 4, they've lost their comrades, and they fight like it: that side flicks twice a turn, with a steadier hand.

Winning

Cross out every enemy soldier.

Tactics

Nobody makes these rules up; they fall out of the ones above. Every one of them was a trick at the back of a classroom first.

  • Stay behind the wall. A wall costs a snipe power and shakes a lunger's hand. Men inside a ring are hard to reach; men standing out on the open paper are free.
  • Look for a row. Two with one bullet: find enemy soldiers standing in a line and you flick again. So never leave yours in one.
  • Bait the lunge. One man stepped just outside your base looks like an easy kill. A lunger who runs through him and lands among the rest gets shot.
  • Catch the convoy. A send is on the open paper for exactly one of your turns. Five walking together is the row you were looking for. Send small, and send behind a base.
  • Soft to finish, hard to gamble. A soft flick is short and sure; save the hard, wild one for when a miss costs nothing.
  • Mind the last four. Get a side down to four and they flick twice, steadier. Take the last few in one turn if you can, or be ready for it.

Still to test

Every number in this book is a guess from the rules lab, where a bot played itself a few thousand times. None of them has been felt at a real table yet.

  • Snipe power loss, 10% a wall and 5% a man. In the lab it keeps snipe streaks short.
  • Two with one bullet, with nothing else holding it back. The longest turns (6 or 7 flicks on average, up to 15) come from lunge chains, not snipes.
  • How far a flick reaches: the same for a snipe and a lunge, from a fifth of the page to a page and a fifth, so the pull is one thing to learn. A small pull is a short line; the short lengths get most of the thumb's travel.
  • Lunge shake at walls and at men, and 3° a link. In the lab, lunges are seven flicks in ten: is cutting through a base too cheap?
  • How far outside the walls you may stand. Inside only changed nothing; further out turned the game into lunges.
  • Sends: one enemy turn on the road, and the limit of 5.
  • Quick at 3 bases of 8: about 16 turns in the lab; Classic about 28.
