# Redesign from scratch: four directions and a pick

The seed doesn't change. Two coloured pens, circles of ten dots, one soldier acts per turn, it shoots or moves, the ink is flicked rather than drawn, anything the ink crosses is dead, and nothing is ever erased. Everything else is open.

The baseline, a ruled exercise book under daylight, is quiet and legible. What it lacks is **place** and **time**. The page floats on a flat brown field, and every moment of play feels the same: the first shot and the last land with the same weight. The four directions below each answer that gap differently.

---

## 1. Lamplight: a war fought after bedtime

A dark desk with one warm lamp. The page is squared maths paper lying on the desk in a pool of tungsten light. The rest of the room is shadow. When you pick up a soldier the camera sits down at the desk: the page tilts away, the grid runs off toward a vanishing point, and your pen stands upright on its dot with a long lamp shadow. Pull back and the pen leans away from your aim while its shadow swings across the paper. Hold it too long and the shadow trembles. Let go and the pen skids: the camera chases the ink head, the page flinches slightly at every soldier it crosses, the pen falls over at the end of its line, and then the camera stands back up. Fresh ink is still wet, so the latest lines catch the lamp and glisten, and they go matte over the next two turns. The paper yellows as the war goes on. In pass-and-play the sheet is **spun round on the desk** to face whoever is holding the phone, the way two kids would share a page. When the last soldier is crossed out, dawn comes through the window: the lamp's warm light gives way to cool morning light, and the winner signs the page.

*Playing it:* intimate and tense. Limited perspective comes from light and a low camera rather than an arbitrary zoom. The page feels like an object on a desk.
*Risk:* a dark scene on a phone in daylight; 3D-ish tilt can disorient; more rendering work per frame. It needs a flat, fully lit overview that is always one gesture away.

## 2. The Record: the page as evidence

Daylight, archival. The page is a sheet from a field ledger: printed grid, a ruled margin column where every turn is logged automatically in typewriter ("T14. RED, NE CAMP. SHOT. 3 STRUCK."), rubber stamps for each casualty count, and paper clips holding the carbon copy each player keeps. Each flick ends with a typed line and a stamp. The end-of-game artifact is a dossier: the map, the log, a casualty ledger and a verdict stamp, exported as a multi-page PDF-like image.

*Playing it:* deliberate and ceremonial, like signing things. The history is legible at a glance.
*Risk:* text-heavy and bureaucratic. It slows play and pulls your eyes off the ink. The flick is the soul of the game, and this direction celebrates the paperwork around it.

## 3. Two Spot: a risograph poster

Loud and graphic. The page is uncoated stock printed in two spot inks, fluorescent pink and federal blue, with visible misregistration and halftone grain. Where lines from both players cross, they overprint into purple. Every flick is a print pass: the drum hum, a hard thunk, the line appears with a slight register shift. Big condensed type, bold motion, bass-heavy sound. The artifact is a poster you'd pin up.

*Playing it:* energetic, punchy, very "festival booth".
*Risk:* it abandons the ballpoint. The seed is a cheap pen on school paper, and that intimacy (the slide of the ball, the pressed dot) is exactly what this would print over. It would also look like a lot of other indie games.

## 4. Loupe: an ant's-eye war

You never see the page whole. You play through a jeweller's loupe at paper-fibre scale: dots are glossy lakes of pigment, ballpoint lines are rivers with a rolling steel ball, the grain of the paper is a landscape. Aiming happens in the lens; the ink runs out of view; you hear what it hit before you see it. The whole sheet is withheld until the war ends, and the final pull-back reveal is the payoff.

*Playing it:* mysterious, disorienting, very much "a weird perspective".
*Risk:* tactics turn into guesswork, the reveal can only happen once per game, and on a phone it tips easily into motion sickness. It's a beautiful demo and a frustrating game.

---

## The pick: Lamplight, with the Record's artifact and the Loupe's withholding

**Lamplight** is the direction that makes the flick itself feel more physical, and the flick is the game. It adds both things the baseline lacks: place (a desk, a lamp, a pen that stands and casts a shadow) and time (wet ink drying, paper yellowing, night turning into dawn). It keeps the ballpoint intimacy that Two Spot throws away, and it gets the Loupe's "you don't get to see everywhere" through a low camera and a pool of light, without taking the whole page away from you.

From **The Record** it takes the artifact: a finished page is signed, filed in a drawer of past wars, and can be replayed as a time-lapse of the whole war drawing itself. From **the Loupe** it takes restraint about what you can see while aiming.

The dark-scene and tilt risks are handled by design:

- The overview is flat and evenly lit, and it's always one tap or pinch away. Planning happens there; the low camera is for committing.
- The lamp never takes any part of the page below readable. Shadow is mood, not fog of war: no rule depends on what's lit.
- A "sit up straight" setting turns the tilt off entirely.

### Why squared paper

The baseline's ruled lines are horizontal only. A grid gives the tilted camera a strong depth cue (the lines converge, so the page reads as 3D immediately). It also gives players something to judge angles against, which suits a game about imprecise aiming. And it's the back page of a maths copy, which is where this game was played.
